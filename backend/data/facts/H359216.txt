The VR Headset System is a three-part, VR and mixed reality (“MR”) system that performs a suite of computing and data processing tasks, including executing applications for entertainment, media, and productivity purposes. The VR Headset System consists of the following three components: (1) The Headset, which is a head-mounted display (“HMD”) that contains lenses and serves as the display of the device. The Headset has user-facing sensors that track the hands, eyes, and face of the user; converts the user’s motions to signals and sends them to the below-described “Puck,” and receives and converts data from the Puck to display on the lenses; (2) The Puck, which conducts the main computing and data processing functions of the VR Headset System. The Puck’s components manage the computing, data processing, storage, graphics processing, WiFi/Bluetooth connectivity, power, and
thermal aspects of the system. It runs a [ ] OS that is pre-installed on the device and stores and executes a library of entertainment and social applications that are installed automatically upon the device set-up, as well as applications that users can create or download from third-party websites following a simple initial verification; and (3) A tether cable that connects the headset to the Puck. All three of the above components must be connected for the VR Headset System to function. The Headset is embedded with firmware that interacts exclusively with the Puck and cannot function independently of the Puck, even if physically connected to other devices. If connected solely to a tablet by a USB-C cable, the Headset could not control or transmit images for display on the tablet, or vice versa. The VR Headset System can be paired and used with standard peripheral devices for computers (e.g., a Bluetooth-enabled keyboard, mouse, gaming controllers, etc.). It can also be connected to separate computing and communications devices such as computers, phones, and gaming consoles, and, when so connected, these devices extend the display of media such as videos, photos, and text from the Headset. The Puck includes the following main components:  The main logic board (“MLB”) that stores applications, runs the OS, performs the computing and data processing functions of the VR Headset System, and executes the applications with which the user can interact through the Headset;  Between 1,100 and 1,200 MLB sub-components, including the following: o A Taiwanese-origin [ ] Chipset System-on-Chip (“SoC”), which is the main processing unit and contains a graphics processing unit that runs the applications and content with which a user interacts; o A Memory/Storage Chip from Korea, Taiwan, or China, which contains non- volatile storage for the OS, applications, and system resources;  One supporting printed circuit board assembly (“PCBA”), mounted with three switches and connectors that interact with the MLB to allow the user to trigger a reset of the device;  Wireless link radio frequency integrated circuits (“RFICs”) that wirelessly connect the Puck to a WiFi network, from which it can download new content and deliver that content to the Headset display. The Puck controls all wireless communications.  Battery and power management integrated circuits (“ICs”) that manage power delivery for the Puck to operate, including when it is disconnected from an external power source. In the Puck, the largest portion of the components’ cost comes from Taiwan, and the largest quantity of the components comes from China. Between 1,100 and 1,200 of the around 1,300 components in the Puck are mounted to the MLB by surface mount technology (“SMT”), which occurs in Vietnam. The most expensive component on the MLB is produced outside of China—namely the [ ] Chipset, which is manufactured in Taiwan. Chinese components account for over 25% of the total MLB components by cost. The components of the Headset include: 2
 Six PCBAs that aggregate inputs from user motions and audio to provide to the Puck for processing, manage sensors that communicate data to the Puck via tether, and receive data from the Puck to display to the user on the lenses;  Between 1,200 and 1,300 PCBA subcomponents and integrated circuits, including: o A Microcontroller Unit (“MCU”) that manages the data aggregation, timing of sensors, and communication of the sensor data from the Headset to the Puck; o Field programmable gate arrays (“FGPAs”), which are flexible integrated circuits that can be programmed and designed to device specifications; o Nordic Radio Frequency (“nRF”)/Bluetooth chips that support the low-power usage of Bluetooth applications; o Light emitting diode (“LED”) drivers that help power the device by converting incoming power as required for the output;  Display and Optical Modules and Lenses that collect data from the PCBAs to project images to the user.  Cameras and Depth Sensors that primarily conduct positional tracking and depth perception functions to allow the device to locate itself within a particular space;  Audio circuitry, including microphones that capture audio and audio transducers that collect data to provide the audio experience for the user via the System PCBAs. In the Headset, the largest portion of both the components’ cost and quantity comes from China. Between 1,200 and 1,300 of the over 1,400 components in the Headset are mounted to the six supporting PCBAs by means of SMT in Vietnam. Other major components, including the display and lenses of the Headset, the camera and depth sensors, and the audio circuitry, allow users to access the content and data generated by the MLB and supported by the seven supporting PCBAs. Together, the system PCBAs account for a majority of the total BOM components by quantity. The tether cable/interconnect is an optical cable that converts electrical signals to fiber and back. The tether connects the Puck and the Headset, and, without the tether, neither component could function. The manufacturing steps for the VR Headset System will occur in four main stages: (1) the production of 2,700 to 2,800 components individual components in 10 different countries including Vietnam, China, Taiwan, the Philippines, Korea, Japan, Singapore, Malaysia, the United States, and Austria ; (2) production of the VR Headset System’s main PCBA (the MLB)—which is in the Puck—in Vietnam by means of SMT, software download and testing of the PCBAs; (3) production of seven Supporting PCBAs in Vietnam by means of SMT, software download, and testing of the PCBAs; and (4) final assembly, final software downloads, testing of the Headset and Puck and packout (“FATP”) of the VR Headset System in China. SMT of the MLB in Vietnam involves incorporating between 1,100 and 1,200 electrical components onto both sides of a bare PCB. Meta states that the MLB executes the computing, data and graphics processing, connectivity, and sensor integration and, therefore, this process creates the “brain” of the VR Headset System. SMT of the MLB line cycle takes over four hours to complete, with a total direct labor requirement of over 20 on-line operators. The importer states that, with few deviations, the SMT processes for the seven supporting PCBAs are similar to the SMT process for the MLB described above. After SMT, the eight Vietnamese-origin PCBAs will be shipped to China for FATP. For the Puck, FATP involves snapping, pasting, bending, and screwing the Puck sub-components and components together. The importer states that this process takes less than 8 minutes, a majority of the assembly is automated, and that none of the steps involved in the Puck FATP will alter the characteristics of the MLB that emerge from SMT in Vietnam. FATP of the Headset takes less than an hour of assembly time, though glue curing time takes significantly longer. FATP of the Headset includes assembly of the camera module, outer frame, optical module, left temple module, right temple module, membrane module, main line assembly, and main line test validation. The importer states that the steps involved in the Headset FATP will not alter the characteristics of the seven supporting PCBAs that emerge from SMT in Vietnam.
